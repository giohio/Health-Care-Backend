**Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain** uses [magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging "Magnetic resonance imaging") (MRI) to produce high-quality two- or three-dimensional images of the [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain "Brain"), [brainstem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainstem "Brainstem"), and [cerebellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebellum "Cerebellum") without [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation "Ionizing radiation") ([X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays "X-rays")) or [radioactive tracers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_tracer "Radioactive tracer").

The first MR images of a human brain were obtained in 1978 by two groups of researchers at [EMI Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMI_Laboratories "EMI Laboratories") led by [Ian Robert Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Robert_Young "Ian Robert Young") and Hugh Clow.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-newscientist1978-1) In 1986, Charles L. Dumoulin and Howard R. Hart at [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric") developed [MR angiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MR_angiography "MR angiography"),[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-popular1987-2); [Denis Le Bihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Le_Bihan "Denis Le Bihan") obtained his first diffusion images and later patented some aspects of [diffusion MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_MRI "Diffusion MRI").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-3) In 1988, [Arno Villringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arno_Villringer "Arno Villringer") and colleagues demonstrated that susceptibility [contrast agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI_contrast_agent "MRI contrast agent") may be employed in [perfusion MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfusion_MRI "Perfusion MRI").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-4) In 1990, [Seiji Ogawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiji_Ogawa "Seiji Ogawa") at [AT&T Bell labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") recognized that oxygen-depleted blood with dHb was attracted to a magnetic field, and discovered the technique that underlies [Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_Magnetic_Resonance_Imaging "Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging") (fMRI).[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Faro2010-5)

[![Image 2](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e9/%27Jedi%27_helmet_Wellcome_L0059902.jpg/250px-%27Jedi%27_helmet_Wellcome_L0059902.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:%27Jedi%27_helmet_Wellcome_L0059902.jpg)

A 'Jedi' helmet, on display at the Science Museum:Medicine:The Wellcome Galleries

In the early 1980s to the early 1990s, 'Jedi' helmets, inspired by the '[Return of the Jedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Jedi "Return of the Jedi")' Star Wars film, were sometimes worn by children in order to obtain good image quality. The copper coils of the helmet were used as a radio aerial to detect the signals while the 'Jedi' association encouraged children to wear the helmets and not be frightened by the procedure. These helmets were no longer needed as MR scanners improved.

In the early 1990s, Peter Basser and Le Bihan, working at [NIH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIH "NIH"), and Aaron Filler, Franklyn Howe, and colleagues developed [diffusion tensor imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_tensor_imaging "Diffusion tensor imaging") (DTI).[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-9) The first DTI Brain Tractogram was published by the United States as Figure 17 of US Patent 5,560,360 (filed March 9, 1993, granted Oct. 1, 1996 - Filler, Howe, Richards & Tsuruda). Joseph Hajnal, Young and [Graeme Bydder](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Graeme_Bydder&action=edit&redlink=1 "Graeme Bydder (page does not exist)") described the use of [FLAIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid-attenuated_inversion_recovery "Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery") pulse sequence to demonstrate high signal regions in normal white matter in 1992.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-10) In the same year, John Detre, Alan P. Koretsky and coworkers developed [arterial spin labeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arterial_spin_labeling "Arterial spin labeling").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-11) In 1997, Jürgen R. Reichenbach, E. Mark Haacke and coworkers at [Washington University in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis "Washington University in St. Louis") developed [Susceptibility weighted imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susceptibility_weighted_imaging "Susceptibility weighted imaging").[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-reichenbach1997-12)

The first study of the human brain at 3.0 [T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_(unit) "Tesla (unit)") was published in 1994,[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-13) and in 1998 at 8 T.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-14) Studies of the human brain have been performed at 9.4 T (2006),[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-15) 10.5 T (2019),[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-16) and up to 11.7 T (2024).[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-17)

[Paul Lauterbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Lauterbur "Paul Lauterbur") and Sir [Peter Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Mansfield "Peter Mansfield") were awarded the 2003 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") for their discoveries concerning MRI.

[![Image 3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/Normal_axial_T2-weighted_MR_image_of_the_brain.jpg/250px-Normal_axial_T2-weighted_MR_image_of_the_brain.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Normal_axial_T2-weighted_MR_image_of_the_brain.jpg)

This axial T2-weighted (CSF white) MR scan shows a normal brain at the level of the lateral ventricles.

The record for the highest spatial resolution of a whole intact brain (postmortem) is 100 microns, from Massachusetts General Hospital. The data was published in _Scientific Data_ on 30 October 2019.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-19)

One advantage of MRI of the brain over [computed tomography of the head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography_of_the_head "Computed tomography of the head") is better tissue contrast,[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-EbelBenz-Bohm1999-20) and it has fewer artifacts than CT when viewing the [brainstem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainstem "Brainstem"). MRI is also superior for [pituitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pituitary "Pituitary") imaging.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-BradleyBrant-Zawadzki2001-21) It may however be less effective at identifying early [cerebritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebritis "Cerebritis").[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-RoosTunkel2010-22)

In the case of a [concussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concussion "Concussion"), an MRI should be avoided unless there are progressive neurological symptoms, focal neurological findings or concern of skull fracture on exam.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-AMSSMfive-23) In the analysis of a concussion, measurements of Fractional Anisotropy, Mean Diffusivity, Cerebral Blood Flow, and Global Connectivity can be taken to observe the pathophysiological mechanisms being made while in recovery.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-24)

In analysis of the [fetal brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetus "Fetus"), MRI provides more information about gyration than [ultrasound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasound "Ultrasound").[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Garel2004-25)

MRI is sensitive for the detection of brain abscess.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Rath_Hughes_Arabi_Shah_2012_pp._585%E2%80%93607-26)

A number of different imaging modalities or [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI_sequence "MRI sequence") can be used with imaging the nervous system:

*   [_T_ 1-weighted (T1W)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_(NMR) "Relaxation (NMR)") images: [Cerebrospinal fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebrospinal_fluid "Cerebrospinal fluid") is dark. [_T_ 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin%E2%80%93lattice_relaxation "Spin–lattice relaxation")-weighted images are useful for visualizing normal anatomy.
*   [_T_ 2-weighted (T2W) images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_(NMR) "Relaxation (NMR)"): CSF is light, but fat (and thus [white matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_matter "White matter")) is darker than with _T_ 1. [_T_ 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin%E2%80%93spin_relaxation "Spin–spin relaxation")-weighted images are useful for visualizing pathology.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-ButlerMitchell2007-27)
*   [Diffusion-weighted images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_MRI "Diffusion MRI") (DWI): DWI uses the diffusion of water molecules to generate contrast in MR images.
*   Proton density (PD) images: CSF has a relatively high level of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton"), making CSF appear bright. [Gray matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_matter "Gray matter") is brighter than white matter.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Tofts2005-28)

[![Image 4](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Brain_MRI_0230_15.jpg/250px-Brain_MRI_0230_15.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Brain_MRI_case_0230 "commons:Category:Brain MRI case 0230")[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Brain_MRI_0230_15.jpg)

[False color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_color "False color") MRI by applying red to T1, green to PD and blue to T2. 

*   [Fluid attenuation inversion recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid-attenuated_inversion_recovery "Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery") ([FLAIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_attenuated_inversion_recovery "Fluid attenuated inversion recovery")): useful for evaluation of [white matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_matter "White matter") plaques near the ventricles.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-ChowdhuryWilson2010-29) It is useful in identifying [demyelination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demyelination "Demyelination").[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Granacher2007-30)

MRI of the brain and head has multiple diagnostic usages, including identifying aneurysms, strokes, tumors and other brain injury.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-31) In many diseases, such as [Parkinson's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson%27s_disease "Parkinson's disease") or [Alzheimer's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer%27s_disease "Alzheimer's disease"), MRI is useful to help [differentially diagnose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_diagnosis "Differential diagnosis") against other diseases.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-33) MRI data has been used with [deep learning networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning "Deep learning") to identify [brain tumors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_tumor "Brain tumor").[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging_of_the_brain#cite_note-Segato_Marzullo_Calimeri_De_Momi_p=041503-34)
