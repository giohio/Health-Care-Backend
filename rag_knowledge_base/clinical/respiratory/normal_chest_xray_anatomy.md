A **chest radiograph**, **chest X-ray** (**CXR**), or **chest film** is a [projection radiograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectional_radiography "Projectional radiography") of the [chest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest "Chest") used to diagnose conditions affecting the chest, its contents, and nearby structures. Chest radiographs are the most common film taken in medicine.

Like all methods of [radiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiography "Radiography"), chest radiography employs [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation "Ionizing radiation") in the form of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") to generate images of the chest. The mean [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation "Radiation") dose to an adult from a chest radiograph is around 0.02 [mSv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sievert "Sievert") (2 [mrem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roentgen_equivalent_man "Roentgen equivalent man")) for a front view (PA, or posteroanterior) and 0.08 mSv (8 mrem) for a side view (LL, or latero-lateral).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-1) Together, this corresponds to a [background radiation equivalent time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_radiation_equivalent_time "Background radiation equivalent time") of about 10 days.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-radiologyinfo-2)

Conditions commonly identified by chest radiography

*   [Pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia")
*   [Pneumothorax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumothorax "Pneumothorax")
*   [Interstitial lung disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_lung_disease "Interstitial lung disease")
*   [Heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_failure "Heart failure")
*   [Bone fracture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture_(bone) "Fracture (bone)")
*   [Hiatal hernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiatal_hernia "Hiatal hernia")
*   [Pulmonary tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_tuberculosis "Pulmonary tuberculosis")

Chest radiographs are used to diagnose many conditions involving the chest wall, including its bones, and also structures contained within the [thoracic cavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoracic_cavity "Thoracic cavity") including the [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung "Lung"), [heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart "Heart"), and [great vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_vessel "Great vessel"). [Pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia") and [congestive heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congestive_heart_failure "Congestive heart failure") are very commonly diagnosed by chest radiograph. Chest radiographs are also used [to screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screening_(medicine) "Screening (medicine)") for job-related lung disease in industries such as mining where workers are exposed to dust.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-3)

For some conditions of the chest, radiography is good for screening but poor for diagnosis. When a condition is suspected based on chest radiography, additional imaging of the chest can be obtained to definitively diagnose the condition or to provide evidence in favor of the diagnosis suggested by initial chest radiography. Unless a fractured rib is suspected of being displaced, and therefore likely to cause damage to the lungs and other tissue structures, x-ray of the chest is not necessary as it will not alter patient management.

The main regions where a chest X-ray may identify problems may be summarized as _ABCDEF_ by their first letters:[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-4)

*   **A**irways, including hilar adenopathy or enlargement
*   **B**reast shadows
*   **B**ones, e.g. rib fractures and lytic bone lesions
*   **C**ardiac silhouette, detecting cardiac enlargement
*   **C**ostophrenic angles, including pleural effusions
*   **D**iaphragm, e.g. evidence of free air, indicative of perforation of an abdominal viscus
*   **E**dges, e.g. apices for fibrosis, pneumothorax, pleural thickening or plaques
*   **E**xtrathoracic tissues
*   **F**ields ([lung parenchyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung_parenchyma "Lung parenchyma")), being evidence of alveolar flooding
*   **F**ailure, e.g. [alveolar air space disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_lung_disease "Alveolar lung disease") with prominent vascularity with or without pleural effusions

Different _views_ (also known as [_projections_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectional_radiography "Projectional radiography")) of the chest can be obtained by changing the relative orientation of the body and the direction of the x-ray beam. The most common views are _posteroanterior_, _anteroposterior_, and _lateral_. In a posteroanterior (PA) view, the x-ray source is positioned so that the x-ray beam enters through the posterior (back) aspect of the chest and exits out of the anterior (front) aspect, where the beam is detected. To obtain this view, the patient stands facing a flat surface behind which is an x-ray detector. A radiation source is positioned behind the patient at a standard distance (most often 6 feet, 1,8m), and the x-ray beam is fired toward the patient.

In anteroposterior (AP) views, the positions of the x-ray source and detector are reversed: the x-ray beam enters through the anterior aspect and exits through the posterior aspect of the chest. AP chest x-rays are harder to read than PA x-rays [_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation\_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] and are therefore generally reserved for situations where it is difficult for the patient to get an ordinary chest x-ray, such as when the patient is bedridden. In this situation, mobile X-ray equipment is used to obtain a lying down chest x-ray (known as a "supine film"). As a result, most supine films are also AP.

Lateral views of the chest are obtained in a similar fashion as the posteroanterior views, except in the lateral view, the patient stands with both arms raised and the left side of the chest pressed against a flat surface.

Required projections can vary by country and hospital, although an erect posteroanterior (PA) projection is typically the first preference. If this is not possible, then an anteroposterior view will be taken. Further imaging depends on local protocols which is dependent on the hospital protocols, the availability of other imaging modalities and the preference of the image interpreter. In the UK, the standard chest radiography protocol is to take an erect posteroanterior view only and a lateral one only on request by a radiologist.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-5) In the US, chest radiography includes a PA and Lateral with the patient standing or sitting up. Special projections include an AP in cases where the image needs to be obtained [stat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_abbreviations_used_in_medical_prescriptions#stat "List of abbreviations used in medical prescriptions") (immediately) and with a portable device, particularly when a patient cannot be safely positioned upright. Lateral decubitus may be used for visualization of air-fluid levels if an upright image cannot be obtained. Anteroposterior (AP) Axial Lordotic projects the clavicles above the lung fields, allowing better visualization of the apices (which is extremely useful when looking for evidence of primary [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis")).

*   [Decubitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decubitus "Decubitus") – taken while the patient is lying down, typically on their side. Useful for differentiating [pleural effusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleural_effusion "Pleural effusion") from consolidation (e.g. pneumonia) and loculated effusions from free fluid in the [pleural space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleural_cavity "Pleural cavity"). In effusions, the fluid _layers out_ (by comparison to an up-right view, when it often accumulates in the [costophrenic angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costophrenic_angle "Costophrenic angle")).
*   [Lordotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lordotic "Lordotic") view – used to visualize the [apex of the lung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apex_of_the_lung "Apex of the lung"), to pick up abnormalities such as a [Pancoast tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancoast_tumor "Pancoast tumor").
*   [Expiratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expiratory "Expiratory") view – helpful for the diagnosis of [pneumothorax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumothorax "Pneumothorax").
*   [Oblique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_projection "Oblique projection") view – useful for the visualization of the ribs and sternum. Although it is necessary to do the appropriate adaptations to the x-ray dosage to be used.

In the average person, the diaphragm should be intersected by the 5th to 7th anterior ribs at the mid-clavicular line, and 9 to 10 posterior ribs should be viewable on a normal PA inspiratory film. An increase in the number of viewable ribs implies hyperinflation, as can occur, for example, with [obstructive lung disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease "Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease") or foreign body aspiration. A decrease implies hypoventilation, as can occur with [restrictive lung disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrictive_lung_disease "Restrictive lung disease"), [pleural effusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleural_effusion "Pleural effusion") or [atelectasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelectasis "Atelectasis"). Underexpansion can also cause [interstitial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_fluid "Interstitial fluid") markings due to parenchymal crowding, which can mimic the appearance of [interstitial lung disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_lung_disease "Interstitial lung disease"). Enlargement of the right descending pulmonary artery can indirectly reflect changes of [pulmonary hypertension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_hypertension "Pulmonary hypertension"), with a size greater than 16 mm abnormal in men and 15 mm in women.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-6)

Appropriate [penetration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penetration_depth "Penetration depth") of the film can be assessed by faint visualization of the thoracic spines and lung markings behind the heart. The right diaphragm is usually higher than the left, with the liver being situated beneath it in the abdomen. The [minor fissure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_fissure "Minor fissure") can sometimes be seen on the right as a thin horizontal line at the level of the fifth or sixth rib. Splaying of the [carina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carina_of_trachea "Carina of trachea") can also suggest a tumor or process in the middle [mediastinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediastinum "Mediastinum") or enlargement of the [left atrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_atrium "Left atrium"), with a normal angle of approximately 60 degrees. The right paratracheal stripe is also important to assess, as it can reflect a process in the [posterior mediastinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_mediastinum "Posterior mediastinum"), in particular the spine or paraspinal soft tissues; normally it should measure 3 mm or less. The left paratracheal stripe is more variable and only seen in 25% of normal patients on posteroanterior views.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-7)

Localization of lesions or inflammatory and infectious processes can be difficult to discern on chest radiograph, but can be inferred by [silhouetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silhouette_sign "Silhouette sign") and the [hilum overlay sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilum_overlay_sign "Hilum overlay sign") with adjacent structures. If either [hemidiaphragm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemidiaphragm "Hemidiaphragm") is blurred, for example, this suggests the lesion to be from the corresponding lower lobe. If the right heart border is blurred, than the pathology is likely in the right middle lobe, though a [cavum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pectus_excavatum "Pectus excavatum") deformity can also blur the right heard border due to indentation of the adjacent sternum. If the left heart border is blurred, this implies a process at the [lingula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingula_(lung) "Lingula (lung)").[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray#cite_note-8)

A [lung nodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung_nodule "Lung nodule") is a discrete opacity in the lung which may be caused by:

*   [Neoplasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplasm "Neoplasm"): benign or malignant
*   Granuloma: [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis")
*   Infection: [round pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_pneumonia "Round pneumonia")
*   Vascular: [infarct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infarct "Infarct"), [varix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esophageal_varices "Esophageal varices"), [granulomatosis with polyangiitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granulomatosis_with_polyangiitis "Granulomatosis with polyangiitis"), [rheumatoid arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatoid_arthritis "Rheumatoid arthritis")

There are a number of features that are helpful in suggesting the diagnosis:

*   rate of growth 
    *   Doubling time of less than one month: sarcoma/infection/infarction/vascular
    *   Doubling time of six to 18 months: benign tumor/malignant granuloma
    *   Doubling time of more than 24 months: benign nodule neoplasm

*   [calcification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcification "Calcification")
*   margin 
    *   smooth
    *   lobulated
    *   presence of a corona radiata

*   shape
*   site

If the nodules are multiple, the differential is then smaller:

*   infection: [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"), fungal infection, [septic emboli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septic_embolism "Septic embolism")
*   neoplasm: e.g., [metastases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastasis "Metastasis"), [lymphoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphoma "Lymphoma"), [hamartoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamartoma "Hamartoma")
*   [sarcoidosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcoidosis "Sarcoidosis")
*   [alveolitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrinsic_allergic_alveolitis "Extrinsic allergic alveolitis")
*   auto-immune disease: e.g., [granulomatosis with polyangiitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granulomatosis_with_polyangiitis "Granulomatosis with polyangiitis"), [rheumatoid arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatoid_arthritis "Rheumatoid arthritis")
*   inhalation (e.g., [pneumoconiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumoconiosis "Pneumoconiosis"))

A cavity is a walled hollow structure within the lungs. Diagnosis is aided by noting:

*   wall thickness
*   wall outline
*   changes in the surrounding lung

The causes include:

*   [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer "Cancer")
*   [infarct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infarct "Infarct") (usually from a pulmonary embolus)
*   infection: e.g., _[Staphylococcus aureus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staphylococcus\_aureus "Staphylococcus aureus")_, [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"), [Gram negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram_negative "Gram negative") bacteria (especially _[Klebsiella pneumoniae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klebsiella\_pneumoniae "Klebsiella pneumoniae")_), anaerobic bacteria, and fungus
*   [Granulomatosis with polyangiitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granulomatosis_with_polyangiitis "Granulomatosis with polyangiitis")

### Pleural abnormalities

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Chest_radiograph&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Pleural abnormalities")]

Fluid in space between the lung and the chest wall is termed a [pleural effusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleural_effusion "Pleural effusion"). There needs to be at least 75 mL of pleural fluid in order to blunt the [costophrenic angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costophrenic_angle "Costophrenic angle") on the lateral chest radiograph and 200 mL of pleural fluid in order to blunt the [costophrenic angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costophrenic_angle "Costophrenic angle") on the posteroanterior chest radiograph. On a lateral decubitus, amounts as small as 50ml of fluid are possible. Pleural effusions typically have a [meniscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meniscus_(anatomy) "Meniscus (anatomy)") visible on an erect chest radiograph, but loculated effusions (as occur with an [empyema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empyema "Empyema")) may have a [lenticular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_(geometry) "Lens (geometry)") shape (the fluid making an [obtuse angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_angle "Obtuse angle") with the chest wall).

Pleural thickening may cause blunting of the costophrenic angle, but is distinguished from pleural fluid by the fact that it occurs as a linear shadow ascending vertically and clinging to the ribs.

The differential for diffuse shadowing is very broad and can defeat even the most experienced radiologist. It is seldom possible to reach a diagnosis on the basis of the chest radiograph alone: high-resolution [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography "Computed tomography") of the chest is usually required and sometimes a lung [biopsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopsy "Biopsy"). The following features are usually noted:

*   type of shadowing (lines, dots or rings) 
    *   reticular (crisscrossing lines)
    *   [companion shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_shadow "Companion shadow") (lines paralleling bony landmarks)
    *   nodular (many small dots)
    *   rings or cysts
    *   ground glass
    *   consolidation (diffuse opacity with air bronchograms)

*   location (where is the lesion worst?) 
    *   upper (e.g., [sarcoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcoidosis "Sarcoidosis"), [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"), [silicosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicosis "Silicosis")/[pneumoconiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumoconiosis "Pneumoconiosis"), [ankylosing spondylitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankylosing_spondylitis "Ankylosing spondylitis"), [Langerhans cell histiocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langerhans_cell_histiocytosis "Langerhans cell histiocytosis"))
    *   lower (e.g., [cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogenic_fibrosing_alveolitis "Cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis"), [connective tissue disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connective_tissue_disease "Connective tissue disease"), asbestosis, drug reactions)
    *   central (e.g., [pulmonary edema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_edema "Pulmonary edema"), [alveolar proteinosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_proteinosis "Alveolar proteinosis"), [lymphoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphoma "Lymphoma"), [Kaposi's sarcoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaposi%27s_sarcoma "Kaposi's sarcoma"), [PCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumocystis_pneumonia "Pneumocystis pneumonia"))
    *   peripheral (e.g., [cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptogenic_fibrosing_alveolitis "Cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis"), [connective tissue disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connective_tissue_disease "Connective tissue disease"), [chronic eosinophilic pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_eosinophilic_pneumonia "Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia"), [bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchiolitis_obliterans_organizing_pneumonia "Bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia"))

*   lung volume 
    *   increased (e.g., [Langerhans cell histiocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langerhans_cell_histiocytosis "Langerhans cell histiocytosis"), [lymphangioleiomyomatosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphangioleiomyomatosis "Lymphangioleiomyomatosis"), [cystic fibrosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cystic_fibrosis "Cystic fibrosis"), [allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergic_bronchopulmonary_aspergillosis "Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis"))
    *   decreased (e.g., fibrotic lung disease, chronic sarcoidosis, chronic extrinsic allergic alveolitis)

[Pleural effusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleural_effusion "Pleural effusion") may occur with cancer, sarcoid, connective tissue diseases and [lymphangioleiomyomatosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphangioleiomyomatosis "Lymphangioleiomyomatosis"). The presence of a pleural effusion argues against pneumocystis pneumonia.

While chest radiographs are a relatively cheap and safe method of investigating diseases of the chest, there are a number of serious chest conditions that may be associated with a normal chest radiograph and other means of assessment may be necessary to make the diagnosis. For example, a patient with an acute myocardial infarction may have a completely normal chest radiograph.
